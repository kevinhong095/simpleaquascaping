The Foundational Four

Overview

When you start aquascaping, few things are as overwhelming as plants. What size? What shape? What color? Where do they go? How much light do they need?

You get the point.

To keep life simple, all of my plants are:

  • Low tech — they'll grow fine without CO2, but thrive consistently with it
  • Slow growing — don't require much maintenance
  • Easy to care for — low risk of dying off
"80% of your results come from 20% of your effort."

This isn't to say there aren't other successful approaches to aquascaping — this is just the one that works best for me. With this system, I'm able to:

  • Build 95%+ of the aquascapes I want with 4 easy plants
  • Keep plants that are easy to use, easy to maintain, and as a result never deal with algae
  • Save hundreds of hours and a lot of money — and ultimately enjoy the hobby more, which is all I care about

Why These Plants Work

Low Algae Risk All of these plants have low light requirements — which makes it hard for algae to get out of control. They also do great in shaded areas, giving you more flexibility in how you design your tank.
Low Maintenance They grow so slowly that maintenance is practically zero. Weekly water changes, a turkey baster to clear detritus off the leaves, a trim every 3–6 months. More time enjoying the tank.
No Aquasoil Needed Aquasoil leaches nutrients into the water during the first month — paired with new plants still transitioning and an unstable tank, that's why most people deal with algae early on. An inert substrate (sand, small pebbles) means one less problem to worry about.
Flexibility Once stem plants are in soil, that's where they stay. Move them and your tank turns into a brown smoothie. With epiphytes, attached with super glue or plant weights, you can move them anywhere at any time with zero issues.
Easy to Find Simple enough that they're available at pretty much every in-person and online retailer. No hunting around.

Basic Principles

  • Buy tissue culture whenever you can. Tissue culture plants are lab-grown, pest-free, and snail-free. For mosses especially, this isn't optional — it's the only way I'd buy them. Online availability is good; local fish stores carry it inconsistently, so call ahead.
  • Plant heavily from the start. A heavily planted tank from day 1 means less free nutrients for algae to feed on. Don't wait until the hardscape looks right — start dense and thin later if needed.
  • These plants don't go in substrate. Epiphytes need to be attached to hardscape, not buried. Burying the rhizome causes rot. Use super glue gel or fishing line to tie them to rocks or driftwood.

The Plants

Ferns

Easiest

Java Ferns and Bolbitis are the most forgiving plants in the hobby. They grow slowly, tolerate a wide range of water parameters, and do well in low light. If you're just getting started, put most of your budget here.

Varieties I recommend
  • Java Fern Narrow
  • Java Fern Narrow 'Mini'
  • Java Fern Philippine
  • Java Fern Philippine 'Mini'
  • Bolbitis Heudelotii
  • Bolbitis Heudelotii 'Mini'
My buying preference

Tissue culture is ideal, but ferns are forgiving enough that emersed-grown specimens do fine. They transition to submerged growth without much drama. If you're buying locally and tissue culture isn't available, emersed ferns are still a good option — just rinse them well before putting them in.

Mosses

Easiest

Most moss sold outside of tissue culture comes with free pests & snails, which is a significant problem and very difficult to deal with after the fact.

My buying preference

Buy tissue culture only. It costs more and can be harder to find, but it's not worth the risk. Do not buy mini coral moss — it's invasive and nearly impossible to remove once it establishes in a tank.

Anubias

Easy

This is my favorite plant group. Anubias are extremely hardy, slow-growing, and pretty much the same across all varieties in terms of care requirements. The differences between varieties are mostly cosmetic — leaf size, shape, texture. Beyond that, treat them all the same.

My buying preference

Tissue culture is preferred. Emersed Anubias are common and transition reasonably well, but tissue culture avoids the snail and pest problem. When buying locally, check that the rhizome is firm and green — soft or brown rhizomes are a sign of rot.

Bucephalandra

Easy-ish

Bucephalandra — "Buce" — is the most delicate of the 4. The variety of colors and textures is unmatched, but it requires patience and the right purchase decision upfront.

My buying preference

First preference is to buy it grown submerged. If you buy emersed-grown Bucephalandra, expect nearly all of it to melt. It will come back — it will just take a long time (typically at least 3+ months, so that's why I recommend getting it grown submerged and waiting 3 months so you have minimal die-off). If submerged isn't available, emersed is fine — just don't do it for your first tank. Wait until you've got a stable system and you're okay watching something melt for a few months before it bounces back.
